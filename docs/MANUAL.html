Support Optimizer

A tool for turning a molecular structure — or any mesh — into something ready to 3D print: it builds or imports the surface, finds and seals interior cavities, searches for the orientation that needs the least support material, and exports a multi-material file a slicer can use.

.pdb / .cif / .stl / .3mf Runs entirely on your machine Desktop app or browser

Overview

What the tool does, end to end.

Molecular structures — proteins, viral capsids, DNA — make striking 3D prints, but the raw data isn't ready to print as-is. Atoms have to become a solid surface; the inevitable internal pockets left by that process become supports a print can't shed; and the structure's natural resting orientation is rarely the one that needs the least scaffolding underneath it. Support Optimizer walks a structure through all three problems in one place, then exports a file your slicer can work with.

The five numbered panels down the left side of the app match the five stages below — everything in the Reference section is organized the same way, so you can jump straight from a control you're looking at to its explanation.

10-Minute Tutorial

One full pass through the pipeline, using SARS-CoV-2 main protease (PDB ID 6LU7) as a concrete example. Any structure works the same way.

1
Load a structure
Section 1 · Fetch from RCSB by ID

Type 6LU7 into the ID field, leave the format on mmCIF and the content on Biological assembly, and click Fetch. The app downloads the structure, voxelizes it, and builds a Solvent Excluded Surface — the same kind of molecular surface tools like PyMOL and ChimeraX render. Watch the status line at the top for progress; a structure this size takes a few seconds.

Crystallographic water is left out automatically. If the file contains ligands or ions, they're listed under Ligands & ions found once loading finishes — all included by default. Uncheck any you don't want printed and click Apply to rebuild.

2
Look around
Section 2 · Scale & view

Drag in the viewport to orbit, scroll to zoom. Pull the Cross-section slider partway across to slice the model open and see its interior directly — this is the fastest way to eyeball whether a structure looks like it has real internal pockets before you go looking for them formally. Reset it (double-click the slider, or the Reset button) when you're done.

3
Find and seal cavities
Section 3 · Voids & cavities

Click Find cavities. The list below fills with every fully-enclosed pocket the structure has, each pre-checked and highlighted on the model itself (magenta = will fill, orange = excluded). These are worth sealing: any support material a slicer generates inside one is permanently trapped once printing finishes. Review the list — for a small, buried pocket there's rarely a reason not to fill it — then click Fill selected cavities.

If you're curious about open channels too (common right at the seam between two touching subunits), click Find tunnels and review that list separately. Nothing there is pre-selected, since filling one is a more deliberate choice than sealing a fully buried pocket. The two lists are independent: finding tunnels doesn't reset your cavity selection, and vice versa.

4
Optimize the orientation
Section 4 · Optimize orientation

Click Optimize orientation. The search tries dozens of candidate orientations and settles on whichever needs the least support material underneath it — you'll see the model rotate and the overhang coloring (red = needs support) shrink. For a model past about 20,000 triangles, the app suggests checking Use low-res proxy first so the search itself doesn't take too long; the rotation it finds is applied losslessly to your full-resolution model either way.

5
See what changed
Section 4 · Show support paths / Compare to start model

Turn on Compare to start model to place your original orientation beside the optimized one, and Show support paths to get a rough, illustrative sense of where supports would go and how much of them there'd be — in Organic (tree-like), Snug or Grid style. This pair of views is the fastest way to confirm the optimization actually helped before you commit to an export.

6
Export
Section 5 · Export STL / Export 3MF

Click Export STL for a standard single-material file, or Export 3MF if you want each chain to land in your slicer as its own selectable part (for multi-filament or multi-resin printing). Both are welded into a proper indexed mesh on export, so a slicer's manifold checker won't flag false "open edges." In the desktop app a Save As dialog lets you choose where the file goes.

Worth knowing

Every mutating step (Scale, Optimize, Fill) can be undone once with the Undo last change button at the top of the panel — but only one step back, not a full history. If you're experimenting, export a copy before trying something you might want to compare against later.

Top of the panel

Two controls that apply to everything, above the numbered sections.

Undo last change

Restores the geometry to exactly how it stood before the last Scale, Optimize, or Fill. One step only — not a full history. Subunit colors and the chain legend come back too, if that operation had changed them.

Colorblind-safe colors

Switches every color that carries meaning to colorblind-safe palettes at once:

  • Chain colors switch to the Tol Muted palette (and back to Default when turned off). If you picked a palette yourself under Scale & view, it's kept.
  • Overhang shading becomes blue / vermillion instead of blue / red.
  • Cavity highlights become bluish green (will fill) / orange (excluded).
  • Axis arrows become orange / reddish purple / sky blue.

It repaints whatever is loaded immediately and applies to anything you load while it's on. Colors that came from an imported 3MF are the file's own and stay as they are — only their overhang marking changes. Chain colors use Tol Muted rather than Okabe–Ito because Okabe–Ito's vermillion is also the overhang color, which would make a sixth chain look like "needs support."

1

Load model

Getting a structure or mesh into the viewer.

PDB/mmCIF surface detail

Sets the voxel grid resolution the molecular surface is built at when you import a .pdb or .cif file — it has no effect on STL or 3MF imports, which already carry their own triangles at whatever resolution the file has.

Auto (the default) scales the grid down as atom count grows, so a very large structure — hundreds of thousands of atoms — still imports in a reasonable time. Pick a fixed value instead if you want consistent detail across structures of different sizes, or want more detail than Auto would choose and are willing to wait longer for it.

Browse for a file…

Loads a local .stl, .pdb, .cif, or .3mf file. You can also just drag a file anywhere onto the viewport.

A 3MF keeps its own colors. They're read either from standard 3MF color groups or from the per-triangle paint data PrusaSlicer, Bambu Studio and OrcaSlicer use — which is also how ChimeraX-Save3MF stores each chain's color. Triangles a slicer's paint tool subdivided more finely than one color per triangle can't be decoded and show as gray.

Fetch from RCSB by ID

Downloads a structure directly from files.rcsb.org by its ID (e.g. 6LU7) — the same file you'd get visiting the URL yourself, no account or API key needed. It's read straight into memory, never saved to disk.

  • mmCIF is the modern format with no per-column width limit, so it can hold structures with tens of thousands of atoms or hundreds of chains that the older format can't. Use this unless you have a specific reason not to.
  • PDB (legacy) is the old fixed-column text format. Fine for small-to-medium structures; large ones may be truncated or simply unavailable in it.

Structure content

  • Biological assembly (default) is the biologically real molecule — what RCSB's own structure page shows. Many entries are deposited as one copy of a repeating unit even though the functional protein is a dimer, trimer or larger; this downloads the already-rebuilt complex. Symmetry copies of a chain arrive suffixed (A, A-2, A-3) so each still gets its own color.
  • Asymmetric unit is the raw deposited file, with no symmetry applied. Pick it if you want exactly what was deposited, or if an entry has no assembly defined.

If a structure looks smaller or is missing subunits compared to the RCSB page, this setting is why.

Build surface from

Appears after a multi-chain PDB/mmCIF loads. Rebuilds the surface from a single chain instead of all of them — useful for printing one protomer of a large complex. It reuses the atoms already read from the file, so nothing is downloaded again. Choose "All chains" and Apply to go back. Rebuilding discards any Fill or Optimize work on the current model.

Ligands & ions found

Appears after a PDB/mmCIF loads, if the file contains any heteroatoms: ligands, metal ions, or leftover crystallization molecules (buffer salts, glycerol, PEG). Each type is listed with its atom count, all checked by default. Uncheck what you don't want in the printed surface and click Apply to rebuild — again from the atoms already read, and combined with whatever chain is selected above.

A ligand bound in a real pocket usually overlaps the protein's own surface and prints fine either way. This list matters most for a loose ion or additive sitting apart from the structure, which would otherwise print as its own tiny floating fragment.

Water is never listed — crystallographic water is always left out, because each water molecule would become a separate speck in the print.

2

Scale & view

Sizing the model and looking at it.

Scale model

Always uniform — the same factor applies to every axis at once, so proportions never distort. Scaling never adds or removes triangles.

geometry.scale(f, f, f) By percentage: f = pct / 100 To target size: f = target / (current size along the chosen axis)

Cross-section

Sweeps a clipping plane along the model's own X axis to reveal what's inside. Purely a view setting — the underlying geometry is never touched by it. Double-click the slider, or use the Reset button beside it, to snap back to fully closed.

Show subunit colors

Available once a multi-chain structure (or a colored 3MF) is loaded. Each chain was voxelized and meshed independently during import — not just recolored afterward — so the boundary between two chains in this view is geometrically exact, not a nearest-atom approximation. A legend below lists each chain and its triangle count.

Overhangs still show in this view: any face past the critical angle is flagged in the same solid overhang color as the plain overhang view. Filling a cavity colors its seal to match the nearest chain, so the colors survive a Fill.

Palette

Which colors chains cycle through. Switching is instant (no rebuild).

  • Default — 12 colors, chosen to avoid the overhang red and blue.
  • Okabe–Ito and Tol Muted — standard colorblind-safe palettes (7 and 9 colors).
  • Viridis — strictly meant for ordered data rather than categories, but colorblind-safe and readable in grayscale, so it's offered too.

A structure with more chains than the palette has colors repeats them. Not available for a 3MF import, whose colors come from the file rather than from chain identity.

3

Voids & cavities

Finding and closing interior empty space, before it becomes trapped support material.

Detection works by voxelizing the current surface, then flood-filling outward from a point guaranteed to be outside the model. Every disconnected pocket of empty space it finds is one of two kinds:

  • Cavity — empty voxels the flood-fill never reaches. Fully enclosed, including a pocket trapped between two touching subunits with no path out.
  • Tunnel — empty voxels the flood-fill does reach, but that still sit within the model's own bounds. An open channel through the part, most often along a subunit seam that never fully closes.

Cavities and tunnels each have their own Find button, list and Fill button. Finding one kind leaves the other list and its selection untouched.

Void detection resolution

The voxel grid resolution detection runs at, shared by both lists. Higher finds smaller and thinner voids — including narrow interface pockets — and shapes their seal more precisely. Since filling only ever adds material inside a void and never re-voxelizes the visible exterior, this setting has no effect on the surface you actually see; it only changes which voids get found.

Cavities

Fully enclosed pockets. Every one found starts pre-checked for filling by default — turn on Custom auto-select size limit to instead pre-check only cavities at or under a voxel-count threshold you choose, hand-tuning individual boxes in the list afterward either way.

Fill selected cavities

Adds a small sealing mesh for each selected cavity directly onto the existing exterior — the exterior itself is never re-voxelized, so the visible surface stays byte-identical.

How deep a seal goes

Right at a thin wall, the detection grid's idea of where the outside begins can be off by about a voxel, so a seal built exactly to the detected boundary could poke through the real surface. To prevent that, the seal is pulled back from the void's edge by a safety margin — and the margin depends on where the void sits:

  • A cavity buried at least 2 voxels from the outside is sealed to its full extent, with no margin. A seal there can't reach the surface.
  • A cavity that may lie close to the outside is sealed with the largest margin that still leaves something to seal: 1 voxel, then ¾, ½, ¼, and finally none.

In practice every selected cavity now gets sealed. (Before v1.0.6 a fixed one-voxel margin applied everywhere, and most thin cavities were skipped as "too thin to seal.")

Tunnels

Open channels. Nothing is pre-selected here — filling a tunnel removes an open channel entirely, a bigger geometric decision than sealing a buried pocket, so review each one's voxel count and rough volume before checking it.

Fill selected tunnels

Builds seals the same way, with one difference: a tunnel always keeps the full one-voxel margin. A tunnel by definition has a real opening to the outside, and sealing it with no margin could close that opening over. The margin pulls the seal back from the opening instead. A tunnel thinner than about three voxels has nothing left after that margin and is skipped — raise the detection resolution and find tunnels again to seal it.

Oversized voids are refused

Each list shows a void's size as a share of the model's own volume. A "tunnel" can sometimes be the entire open space between several distant chains rather than a local gap — filling that would bolt a patch roughly the size of the model onto it. Fill refuses any void at or above 20% of the model's volume and says why; those are flagged in the list.

Highlight on model

Draws a small standalone mesh for each of the 20 largest cavities and 20 largest tunnels — literally remeshing that void's own enclosed voxels — colored magenta if it will be filled, orange if excluded (bluish green / orange with colorblind-safe colors on). The outer shell turns semi-transparent while this is on, since these highlights sit fully inside solid material and would otherwise render hidden behind it.

4

Optimize orientation

Searching for the resting orientation that minimizes support material.

Critical overhang angle

A face counts as needing support once it leans past this many degrees from vertical, measured from its own surface normal:

lean° = asin(clamp(−normalZ, −1, 1)) × 180/π

0° is a vertical wall (self-supporting); 90° is a flat, downward-facing overhang, the worst case. This single number drives the overhang coloring, the support-path preview, and the cost function Optimize searches on. Set it to match your printer and material: most FDM machines manage about 45°, resin printers often more.

Optimize orientation

Samples roughly 32 points spread evenly over a sphere (a Fibonacci sphere) plus the 6 axis-aligned directions — mechanical and molecular parts often have their true optimum sitting exactly on one of those, which sphere sampling alone can miss — scores every candidate, refines a small grid around the best few, and picks the winner. The sphere sample is randomly rotated on every run, so how well the search does doesn't depend on how the structure happens to be oriented in its file; the 6 axis directions stay fixed.

cost = Σ over overhang triangles of area × (drop height to the plate, or to whatever surface occludes it first)

The score also subtracts a build-plate contact-area term, weighted at roughly 15% of the model's bounding radius — without it, a corner-balanced orientation can tie a flat, stable one, since both can show zero triangles past the critical angle even though only one of them would actually sit still on a print bed.

Use low-res proxy for orientation search

The search evaluates cost for dozens of candidate orientations, and each evaluation costs roughly the square of the triangle count — a 200,000-triangle mesh means on the order of 4×1010 operations per candidate, infeasible directly. This voxelizes the mesh at a coarser resolution and searches a remesh of that instead. The result is just a rotation, so it's exact and lossless to apply to your full-resolution model afterward — only the search itself is approximate, never the final geometry. The app suggests it once a model passes 20,000 triangles.

Show support paths · Style

An illustrative visualization, not a real slicer simulation, and never part of the export. All three styles start from the same overhang triangles, grouped into "roots", each traced straight down to where it lands — the plate, or another part of the model underneath (bridging).

  • Organic (tree-like) — roots merge pairwise as they descend, like tree supports fusing into fewer trunks near the bed. Every connecting segment stays within the critical overhang angle of vertical, since a support branch is printed the same way the part is; a pair that can't merge within that angle goes to the plate separately.
  • Snug — the same roots, closely following the overhang's shape, each drawn as its own straight vertical column.
  • Grid — roots snapped to a regular lattice, each its own straight column — a blockier, more generic pattern.

Runs in the background; skipped only above 3,000,000 triangles.

Compare to start model

Places a copy of the model as it was when you loaded it beside the current one, offset by a gap proportional to the model's own width. The difference shows everything you've done since — scaling, filling and optimizing. Undo doesn't move this baseline; only loading a new model does. Combine it with the support-path view to compare support before and after.

5

Export

Getting a slicer-ready file out.

Exports always use the model's real orientation — the one from Optimize, or wherever you last left it. Drag-rotating the view is for looking only and never changes what gets saved. In the desktop app, both exports open a Save As dialog; in a browser, the file goes to your usual downloads folder.

Raise base to Z

Offsets the exported copy's Z so its lowest point sits at this height instead of 0 — the model on screen is never touched, since Optimize and Fill both depend on it always resting at Z=0. Meant for a breakaway support or interface layer you'll print as a separate raft below. Many slicers auto-drop imported objects back onto the bed regardless of a file's own coordinates — check your slicer's placement behavior if the offset doesn't seem to stick, and use the Preview button to see the raise applied to the live view first.

Export STL

Every mesh this app builds internally is a non-indexed triangle soup — geometrically closed, but with no triangle sharing a vertex with its neighbor. Left as-is, some slicers' manifold checkers misread that as open edges even though nothing is actually missing. Export welds the geometry by position into a properly indexed mesh first — a boundary between two differently-colored regions stays deliberately split, since a single vertex can't be two colors at once.

Export 3MF (multi-material)

Same welding as STL export, but 3MF also carries color: triangles are grouped into separate materials and separate objects, so a slicer's object list shows every part individually — selectable, assignable to its own filament or resin.

With subunit colors showing, that's one object per chain; the overhang marking visible on screen is left out on purpose so it can't split a chain into meaningless extra parts. With overhang colors showing instead, export groups by those (typically two objects: needs support / self-supporting).

Concepts

The ideas behind the controls, for when "what does this actually compute" comes up.

Solvent Excluded Surface (SES)
The molecular surface this app builds from a PDB/mmCIF import — the same kind PyMOL and ChimeraX render by default. Computed via a two-pass Euclidean distance transform over a voxelized union of atomic spheres, offset by a solvent probe radius (1.4Å, water, by default): roughly, the surface a water molecule would trace rolling across the structure, which smooths over the small gaps between adjacent atoms that a raw van der Waals surface would leave as sharp reentrant crevices.
Heteroatoms
Atoms in a structure file that aren't part of the protein or nucleic-acid chains themselves: bound ligands, metal ions, and molecules left over from crystallization. Water is the most common by far and is always excluded here; the rest are listed under Ligands & ions found for you to keep or drop.
Marching cubes
The algorithm that turns a voxel grid (values at a regular 3D lattice of points) into an actual triangle mesh, by looking at each small cube of 8 neighboring grid points at a time and triangulating however the solid/empty boundary passes through it. Used both for building the initial surface and for remeshing small void components when sealing them.
Voxel grid resolution
How finely a shape gets rasterized before marching cubes or flood-fill runs over it — expressed as the voxel count along the model's longest axis. Higher resolution resolves finer detail (thinner walls, smaller pockets) at the cost of roughly cubic growth in compute and memory; every resolution control in the app exists because there's a real detail/speed tradeoff being made, not an arbitrary default.
Cavity vs. tunnel
Both are "voids" — disconnected pockets of empty space a flood-fill finds inside the model. A cavity is one the flood-fill can never escape from (fully enclosed); a tunnel is one it can (an open channel to the outside somewhere). See Voids & cavities for how each gets handled.
Seal margin
How far a seal is pulled back from a void's detected edge, so it can't poke through the real surface where the detection grid misjudged a thin wall. It's measured as a true straight-line distance (a Euclidean distance transform), so it can be a fraction of a voxel. Cavities buried well inside get no margin; cavities near the outside get the largest margin that still leaves something to seal; tunnels always keep one voxel. See the callout under Fill selected cavities.
Orientation cost
The single number Optimize minimizes: total overhang area weighted by how far each overhang triangle would have to drop before something (the plate, or another part of the model) is underneath it, minus a term rewarding a larger flat contact area with the plate. Lower is better; it's not a physical unit like grams of support material, just a consistent way to rank orientations against each other.
Support paths
A rough, illustrative sketch of where supports would likely go — not a real slicer's support generator. Real tools (PrusaSlicer's Organic supports, Cura's Tree supports) do full collision avoidance against the model as branches grow and are tuned against real print behavior; this view exists to compare relative support burden between two orientations at a glance, not to be printed from directly.

Tips & Best Practices

Working with very large structures

Leave the import resolution on Auto, and check Use low-res proxy before running Optimize once the model is past roughly 20,000 triangles (the app will suggest this automatically). Both keep the heavy O(n²)-class computations — direct orientation cost evaluation especially — off a full-resolution mesh where they'd be impractically slow, without changing the final exported geometry at all.

Small floating pieces in the slicer

Look at Ligands & ions found in section 1: a loose ion or crystallization additive sitting apart from the structure prints as its own tiny fragment. Uncheck it and Apply. (Water, the most common cause, is already excluded.)

If a tunnel gets skipped

A "too thin to seal" message now only happens for tunnels thinner than about three detection voxels. Raise Void detection resolution a tier or two and run Find tunnels again.

Undo is one step

If you're trying a few different Fill or Optimize choices to compare, export a copy of each result you might want to keep before moving on — Undo only reverses the single most recent mutating operation.

FAQ

Does any of my data leave my computer?

No, except the structure fetch itself when you use "Fetch from RCSB by ID" — that's a direct download from files.rcsb.org, the same request your own browser would make visiting the URL. A locally loaded file never leaves your machine, and nothing about your session is saved anywhere once you close the app.

My slicer says the exported file has open edges — is something wrong?

Unlikely — see Export STL. The app welds the mesh into a properly indexed form specifically to avoid this, but a slicer's manifold checker can occasionally still be strict about very thin, sharp features regardless of source. If it persists, try raising the resolution the affected region was built or sealed at.

Why didn't Fill change how the model looks?

By design — Fill only ever adds material fully inside an existing cavity or tunnel, never touches the visible exterior. The change is real (check the triangle count and the status message after filling) but everything it added is, by construction, invisible from outside.

Optimize picked an orientation that still shows red (overhang) triangles — is that a bug?

No — the search minimizes total weighted overhang cost across the whole model, not the count of overhung triangles. An orientation with a few small, low, unavoidable overhangs can beat one with zero overhangs but poor plate contact (see the cost formula under Optimize orientation), since a real print also needs to sit stably on the bed.

A tunnel I found is enormous — should I fill it?

Usually not, and above 20% of the model's volume the app won't let you. The cavity/tunnel split is purely topological (does the flood-fill reach the outside or not), so a single "tunnel" can be the entire open space between several distant chains rather than a small local gap. A tight seam-level tunnel is usually worth sealing; a huge one spanning most of the interior usually isn't.

I loaded a 3MF and it's all gray.

The file either has no color information, or stores it as paint data a slicer subdivided more finely than one color per triangle, which this app can't decode. Files from ChimeraX-Save3MF and 3MFs with standard color groups load in their own colors.

The colorblind-safe option didn't change my 3MF's colors.

By design: a 3MF's colors are the file's own and represent what will be printed, so they're left alone. Only the overhang marking switches.
